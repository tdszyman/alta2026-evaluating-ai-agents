## Running example

One made-up trace runs through the deck, so the audience builds one judge for one case. A career assistant is asked for **weekend** barista jobs. It calls `get_profile()` (Fitzroy), then `search_jobs(role="barista", near="Fitzroy")`, which drops “weekend”. One of the three results is a weekday job, and the reply calls all three “weekend jobs”. Every code check passes (section 1); two judge criteria fail (Steps 1–4, the worked example in 3.5); Step 5 checks the judge (4.3). The trace is not one of the hands-on traces. All judge outputs on the slides are labelled illustrative.

## Deck order (draft)

Sources in brackets are the ones cited on the slide; the presenter notes cite more.

0. **Opening.**
    1. “Our running example: a career assistant”: one abstract line drawing across the slide, read left to right, coloured by role like the trace on the next slide (the user, assistant and tool colours). A job seeker (a bust with short hair) says the request in a speech bubble, with weekend, barista and near me in bold over a calendar, a cup and a map pin. The career assistant is a phone joined to its two tools, `get_profile` and `search_jobs`, each with a one-line description. A line under the drawing says the conversation is made up and comes back in sections 1, 3 and 4. The slide does not build. It replaced the outline slide on 11 Nov, at Parvin's request; she then asked for the picture to be more abstract and almost without colour, for a better visual with everything appearing with a single click, for a different shape of person, and for lines coloured like the next slides (11 Nov).
    2. “You be the judge”: the audience scores the running-example reply 1–5 by show of hands; then the tool calls are highlighted (Zheng et al. 2023).
1. **Deterministic evaluation** (learning goal 1; program topics: deterministic evaluation, and deterministic vs model-based).
    1. Divider.
    2. 1.1 “Deterministic checks are exact, cheap and repeatable”: what code can check in a trace, why teams start there, and the price: the right answer must be fixed in advance (Shankar et al. 2024; Winston 2026).
    3. 1.2 “Tool calls: the right tool, valid parameters”: three checks on one call; single-call vs multi-turn and missing-value accuracy on BFCL (Patil et al. 2025).
    4. 1.3 “Task completion: check the system, not the reply”: a reply that says done against a failing state check; final state, tests and exact answers; one run is not a measurement (Winston 2026; Yao et al. 2024; Sternbach 2026; Mialon et al. 2024).
    5. 1.4 “Every code check passes, and the answer is still wrong”: pass/fail checks on the running example, and where code stops (Patil et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024).
    6. 1.5 “Deterministic or model-based: side by side”: what each checks and needs, repeatability, how each errs (too strict, too lenient), cost per check, latency and upkeep; use both (Yao et al. 2024; Winston 2026; Ma et al. 2024; Lù et al. 2025; Kwok et al. 2026).
    7. 1.5 “Rules and judges miss different things”: on web-agent runs, rules are precise and judges find more successes; on one QA set, exact match agreed with people better than a GPT-4 judge (Lù et al. 2025; Verga et al. 2024).
2. **LLM as a judge: the idea and its failure modes** (learning goal 2; program topics: LLM-as-a-judge, and a judge's latency and cost).
    1. Divider.
    2. 2.1 “An LLM judge reads meaning, at the cost of a model call”: criteria + trace → judge → verdict; single-answer, pairwise and reference-guided; give it something to check against (Zheng et al. 2023). Part 3 uses single-answer grading.
    3. 2.2 “Judges match people on some tasks, not all”: chat vs coding traces vs web traces (Zheng et al. 2023; Peng et al. 2026; Lù et al. 2025; Bavaresco et al. 2025).
    4. 2.3 “How judges fail, and the design response”: bias, leniency, blind spots, inconsistency, rationalisation, drift and manipulation (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024; Lù et al. 2025; Jia et al. 2025; Kwok et al. 2026; Winston 2026; Liu et al. 2023; Raina et al. 2024).
    5. 2.4 “Count the calls before you run”: the Part 3 app's 10 or 50 calls; every fix multiplies the calls; longer traces cost more per call (workshop README; Zheng et al. 2023; Kwok et al. 2026; Sternbach 2026; Parnin et al. 2023).
    6. 2.4 “Where the judge runs sets the budget”: development, release gate, monitoring and guardrail, each with what runs, its latency, its cost and what fits (Winston 2026; Mohammadi et al. 2025).
    7. 2.4 “Cheaper judges can still be good judges”: a jury of three small models against GPT-4 Turbo; an open 13B judge given a rubric and a reference; check a cheaper judge before switching (Verga et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2024a; Kim et al. 2024b).
3. **Rubrics, scales and prompt patterns** (learning goal 3; program topic: criteria, rubrics, scales, prompting). A short lecture (3.1–3.3) and tips (3.4), then the worked example (3.5), in which Steps 1–4 build the running-example judge, one Part 3 page at a time.
    1. Divider.
    2. 3.1 “A rubric spells out what ‘good’ means”: the five parts of a rubric; broad ratings or specific behaviours; one rubric for every case or one per case; three tests of a usable criterion (Winston 2026; Finch et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2026; Arora et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Peng et al. 2026).
    3. 3.2 “Pick the simplest scale, then define every level”: four kinds of scale, simplest first, with 1–5 marked as the Part 3 scale; a pass mark per criterion; anchors for every level; whole-number scores tie close cases (Winston 2026; Shankar et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2024a; Kwok et al. 2026).
    4. 3.3 “Prompt patterns, and the evidence behind each”: eight patterns in one table (task framing, one criterion at a time, anchored levels, decision rules, something to check against, examples, reason then score, cited evidence), each with what the prompt does and the evidence for it; most measured effects are a few points (Raina et al. 2024; Peng et al. 2026; Kim et al. 2024a; Upadhyay et al. 2024; Winston 2026; Zheng et al. 2023; Siro et al. 2026; Liu et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2026; Gu et al. 2024).
    5. 3.4 “Tips for criteria and scales”: start from failures you have seen; ask a question about the trace; pair criteria that pull apart; keep the list short; start with yes/no; anchor each level with a real case; weight by probability if the API allows; tie the pass mark to a decision (Shankar et al. 2024; Winston 2026; Ruan et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Upadhyay et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Le et al. 2026).
    6. 3.4 “Tips for judge prompts”: rubric first and the trace fenced as data; give it the facts; ask for evidence code can check; no model ranking its own output; pin the model and prompt; temperature 0 is not reliability; tune on one set and report on another; log tokens and latency (Panickssery et al. 2024; Winston 2026; Upadhyay et al. 2024; draft Part 3 judge).
    7. 3.5 Step 1. “Replace ‘helpful’ with criteria tied to the trace”: the four targets of the Part 3 app pinned to the trace, each meeting the tests from 3.1 (Winston 2026; Jia et al. 2025).
    8. 3.5 Step 2. “Write an anchor for every level”: the running example's anchored 1–5 ladder for use of tool results, with an edge-case rule and pass mark 4, beside the Part 3 app's generic draft anchors (draft Part 3 judge, `workshop/prompts/judges.json`).
    9. 3.5 Step 3. “Write the prompt with tested patterns”: a judge prompt for one criterion, its five parts labelled with the patterns from 3.3 (Liu et al. 2023; Winston 2026; draft Part 3 judge).
    10. 3.5 Step 4. “The judge returns a score with evidence”: illustrative output that cites message IDs; the hands-on rule that a malformed result is an evaluation error, not a low score (Winston 2026).
4. **Domain-specific criteria and human review** (learning goal 4; program topics: when teams choose rules vs judges vs humans, and the quality / latency / reliability / cost trade-offs).
    1. Divider.
    2. 4.1 “Domain criteria come from the task, failures and experts” (Yao et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Arora et al. 2025; Siro et al. 2026).
    3. 4.2 “Policy and precedent: a domain judge for job search”: LinkedIn's relevance judge, calibrated against experts (Le et al. 2026).
    4. 4.3 Step 5. “Check the judge against people”: label a sample, read the evidence, rerun and swap, lock a version; why raw agreement misleads (Szymanski et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2026; Shankar et al. 2024; Le et al. 2026).
    5. 4.4 “Choose code, a judge or a person by what the check needs”, with each method's trade-off and the rule of thumb to layer them (Winston 2026; Voss 2026).
    6. 4.5 “Quality, latency and cost, method by method”: code, a large LLM judge, a small distilled judge and people, with figures where a source measured them; accuracy alone can rank the wrong option first (Arora et al. 2025; Le et al. 2026; Chiang and Lee 2023; Kapoor et al. 2024).
    7. 4.5 “In practice, teams layer code, judges and people”: code on every trace, a judge for meaning, people on a sample and the hard cases, with the calibration loop; how teams keep it affordable: route the unsure cases to people, distil for volume, sample live traffic (Voss 2026; Winston 2026; Wang et al. 2024; Le et al. 2026).
5. **Close.**
    1. “Recap, and what Part 3 needs”: one card per learning goal, each with the Part 3 page it prepares (Patil et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2023; Winston 2026; Arora et al. 2025 to read first).
    2. Next.
